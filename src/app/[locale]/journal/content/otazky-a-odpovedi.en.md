### Free will, karma, and the divine plan

The first part of the question consists of two different parts. The first concerns what Pavlína raised: if a person creates his own reality, as Gurudev often says — that you are the creator of your own happiness and unhappiness — then in the present situation, in reality, that situation does not come from outside, but you yourself are its creator. As Gurudev often repeats. Then, under such conditions, where we have our own free will, and if there is some plan that has been set for us, where we are meant to play some role in the future, what is the subtle boundary between the two? How can we recognize whether something is karma, created by us, and the consequence of karma, or whether it is the consequence of some divine plan, through which nature gives us some task and directs our life and its state in that direction? How can we understand that difference? That is the question, Gurudev.

“Very beautiful. It seems you have devoted quite a lot of time to this.” “The point is that whatever exists in the world, whatever concerns us, concerns us ourselves — its creators and its destroyers are we ourselves. That is the basic rule. We have a greater power to create problems, but a somewhat lesser power to find solutions. And that is precisely because our attention turns again and again toward problems. That is why we see problems everywhere, we see only destruction. If we turn this view toward solutions, keep it focused on creation, and keep it positive — then it becomes so through drashtā bhāva, the attitude of the observer.”

There is only one way. There is no other way in the world: through sākshī bhāva, the attitude of the witness, through your attitude as observer. Then you clearly see who creates — whether it is I, or whether something comes from outside. On the day you see this completely clearly and truly become the observer, then you will clearly see the creation and destruction that you yourself cause. And that is precisely the solution. Nothing comes from outside. Nothing goes out. Everything is inside and will remain inside. There is no way it could go outside. “So, Rajeev ji, the question is formulated very simply, and its solution is equally simple. When you look at everything through drashtā bhāva — the attitude of the observer — everything begins to regulate itself. You don’t need to control anything. Everything works like a mechanism; it is self-regulating.

And everything you see is precisely māyā. What is visible is māyā. It changes at every moment, changing its form with every moment. You stand on the riverbank — the water has flowed away. You turn your head this way and look again. The river is still the same, but the water you saw before has already flowed away. Likewise the air flows — and is gone. You breathe in once and breathe out, and meanwhile the air that was here has already moved on. Everything changes at every moment. If drashtā bhāva is present, then you understand all of this, you see it. And then you understand that we too are part of prakriti — nature. At present we consider that nature is here for us, that we have come to use this nature. That this nature exists only for man. We forget that man too is part of this nature. When you separate man out, nature becomes incomplete. Nature therefore includes everyone together, as a whole, in totality, including man. That is why we are able, through drashtā bhāva, to observe everything in nature very well and to see that we are part of it. That is why it is necessary always to keep in mind that whatever creation, destruction, or establishment of some rule or principle takes place, it will be determined precisely by your drashtā bhāva. That is why drashtā bhāva must be kept strong. That is the only means for solving every one of your problems. Whenever a problem or question arises before you, first go deep into its essence. Try to look at it from a distance, in the attitude of the observer. See whether what we are doing, we are doing under the influence of fear and greed, or whether we are doing it for the good of all. Whether we are doing it for the benefit of humanity and the whole of creation. Then you will see it. If you are doing it for yourself, for a few of your close people, acquaintances, friends, or family — if you are doing it for them — then it is your own benefit and gain. But if you are doing it for the totality of the whole creation, of which you yourself are a part, if you want to do it for that, and think about it in this way, then it will proceed in accordance with nyāya, dharma, and satya — justice, righteousness, and truth. So, according to what your drashtā bhāva is, you will see how things arrange themselves and how they appear before you on their own. But when you look through drashtā bhāva, then it truly is very remarkable. It is precisely on this that the greatest attention needs to be focused.”

“Is there any part of the question left? In my view, the solution lies in exactly this. The solution lies in exactly this.”

“It is entirely on the level of principle. When we now look at this plan in such a way that we separate ourselves from nature, we do not become part of it, even though we are part of nature.” “The answer is already contained in that, Gurudev, in your explanation.” “Good, Gurudev, exactly so.”

### What happens to consciousness in the transition from meditation to sleep?

“Or more precisely: what happens to consciousness? “Meditation is a wakeful state.” “How could someone who is awake pass into sleep?” “So how could someone who is awake, who has already gained experience, now do childish things?” “And so, through these childish activities, one gradually grows, gains more and more experience, rises to its peak, and can then enter meditation, can enter samādhi.” “So all these things we are trying to understand — there is a means to understand them. “On the day he understands that he is doing something wrong, he loses the courage to continue doing it.” “That is precisely the moment. That is why there is freedom in performing karma and unfreedom in experiencing its fruits. It has been established this way and will always be so, as long as the human race exists. Good. Pavlína writes very good questions. It is a good discussion.” “If anything remains unanswered, anything at all, Pavlína can point it out. ### Quantum physics, māyā, and Brahman

### The ten activities of a human being and the path to complete knowledge

“In impressions, the mind is fully present: āsvādana — tasting or savoring — and cayana — selecting. “From chitta you took imagination — chitraṇa — from the causal body.” “And now comes your vritti.” “It lies between the mind and chitta. ### The Ten Activities — a complete corrected overview and glossary

Based on a conversation recorded in GMT20260920-161948_Recording.m4a on September 20, 2026. Processed on September 21.

Complete English translation of the corrected material. Contains an overview, an explanation of the 4½ + 5½ division, a comparison with classical terminology, and a glossary. This is a study aid, not a verbatim transcript of the conversation.

**1. Main corrections.** The word previously transcribed as “mislešan” is vishleshaṇa — विशेषण — analysis. The new automatic transcription of the recording recognizes this term at approximately 1:16:59–1:17:15, where it forms a pair with tulan / tulanā — comparison. In the following explanation the English word “analyze” is also heard. Standard spelling is independently confirmed by published texts. The earlier “testing and selecting” is more accurately translated as “tasting or savoring, and selecting.” The corresponding Hindi term is āsvādana — आस्वादन. The speaker explains it using the example of a remembered taste and the selection of food from a particular shop. Automatic transcriptions render the spoken English as “testing,” but the Hindi term and the examples support the meaning “tasting,” not technical testing.

**2. Five domains and ten activities.** The following order corresponds to the previous overview. This is an assignment of functions within this teaching, not an anatomical location or a claim about specific brain regions. Manas — मनस्, in Hindi man, मन — includes āsvādana (आस्वादन): tasting, savoring, sensory experiencing, and cayana (चयन): selecting, choosing. Something is experienced, and on that basis we choose — an example is choosing food based on a remembered taste. Chitta — citta, चित्त — includes cintana (चिन्तन / चिंतन): thinking, contemplation, and chitraṇa (चित्रण): imagining, forming mental images. This is thinking about what something is and what it is like, and imagining possibilities using memory, for example the colors of a room. Vritti — vṛtti, वृत्ति — includes vishleshaṇa (विशेषण): analysis, and tulana/tulanā (तुलन / तुलना): comparing, assessing — according to certain criteria: preference and personal benefit, or justice, dharma, and truth. Buddhi — बुद्धि — includes bodha (बोध): comprehension, knowing, and saṅkalpa (संकल्प): decision, intention, resolve. Out of comprehension comes firm resolve: “This is how it is; this is what I will do.” Ātman — ātman, आत्मन्, in Hindi ātmā, आत्मा — includes anubhava (अनुभव): experience, direct perceiving or knowing, and pramāṇa (प्रमाण): confirmation, verification. The speaker connects experience with its confirmation; “pramāṇa” here should not automatically be understood as scientific proof. Five domains times two activities equals ten activities. The distinction between āsvādana at the level of manas and anubhava at the level of ātman is essential in this explanation — translating both simply as “experience” would obscure this distinction.

**3. How 4½ + 5½ fits together.** Counted in 4½: both activities of manas (āsvādana + cayana = 2), chitraṇa from chitta (= 1), vishleshaṇa and half of tulana from vritti (= 1½) — total four and a half. Counted in the remaining 5½: cintana from chitta (= 1), the other half of tulana (= ½), both activities of buddhi (bodha + saṅkalpa = 2), and both activities of ātman (anubhava + pramāṇa = 2) — total five and a half. The first half of comparison is described using the terms priya–hita–lābha: what is pleasant or dear, what serves a certain interest or benefit, and what brings profit. The second half is described using the terms nyāya–dharma–satya: justice, dharma, and truth. “Half” is part of the explanatory scheme, not a precisely measured division of a psychic faculty. The earlier formulation narrowed priya–hita–lābha too much to “people close to me.” The speaker does give family and friends as examples, but the terms themselves also include preference, benefit, and gain.

Note on an inconsistency in the text: the original transcript explicitly places imagining in the 4½ and reserves contemplation for the 5½. The new speech recognition captures *cintana* in the recap around 1:19:25–1:19:35. However, a second recognition model also captures *chitraṇa* in a later recap around 1:19:49–1:20:07. This may be a slip of the tongue or a recognition error; it cannot simply be attributed to an old translation. The overview above is an editorially unified summary based on the explicit distinction and on the total count of activities. It should not be presented as though every repetition in the recording is entirely identical.

The conversation connects these activities to the following ways of living: 4½ → saṃvedanā → bhoga — sensory feeling and enjoyment; a life in which one’s own needs and preferences are in the foreground. 5½ → saṃjñāna / saṃjñānitā → tyāga — cognition or awareness, which the speaker connects with renunciation and a broader focus of life. 4½ + 5½ = 10 → mānavīyatā → wholeness → sadupayoga — humanity, or full human living, which is expressed through proper use. These connections reflect the speaker’s explanation and are not universal dictionary definitions of these terms.

The goal is not to discard the first 4½ activities and live only through the remaining 5½. The speaker recommends living through all ten. He calls such a person a yogi and emphasizes sadupayoga — proper, beneficial use. The practical meaning is explained by his examples: wear the clothing that is needed; use what is necessary and share what remains; use a needle where a needle is needed, and a sword where a sword is needed; depending on the task and circumstances, choose a helicopter, a private plane, an ox-cart, or a bicycle. The emphasis is on proper use, not on attachment exclusively to enjoyment or exclusively to renunciation. The earlier scheme placed samyak between bhoga and tyāga as proper and balanced use between enjoyment and renunciation — this should be understood as an illustrative interpretation. Samyak means correctly, properly, or fully; “the middle between enjoyment and renunciation” is not its only literal meaning. The precise use of this word elsewhere in the recording was not independently verified from the audio during this review.

**4. Comparison with classical yoga and Vedānta.** The scheme of ten activities should be distinguished from the common fourfold Vedāntic description of the antaḥkaraṇa — the “inner instrument”: manas — attending to information and weighing it, considering alternatives or doubting; buddhi — discerning, understanding, and deciding; ahaṃkāra — the sense of “I,” identifying with experience as something “mine”; chitta — memory and recollection, in some descriptions contemplation. These are functional aspects of the inner instrument, not necessarily four separate organs, and terminology differs between traditions — in particular, chitta has a broader meaning in Patanjali’s yoga than mere “memory.” In Patanjali’s yoga, moreover, vritti denotes the activities or modifications of chitta, and the five kinds given in Yoga Sutra 1.6 are pramāṇa, viparyaya, vikalpa, nidrā, and smṛti — valid cognition, misconception, conceptual construction, sleep, and memory — which is a different division from the five domains with two activities each given above.

**5. Glossary.** Ātman (in Hindi ātmā) — Self; in this conversation connected with anubhava and pramāṇa. Anubhava — experience, direct perceiving or knowing. Anubhūti — experience or inner experience/perception, related in meaning to anubhava; a more precise distinction depends on how the speaker uses the terms. Pramāṇa — proof, confirmation, or means of valid cognition; here the speaker connects it with confirming experience. Manas (in Hindi man) — mind, in this model the domain of āsvādana and cayana. Āsvādana — tasting, savoring, appreciating through experiencing; the missing Hindi counterpart of the earlier “testing.” Cayana — selection, choosing. Buddhi — understanding or discerning intelligence, connected with bodha and saṅkalpa. Bodha — comprehension, awareness, knowing. Saṅkalpa — intention, resolve, firm decision. Ṛtambharā (probable reading) — bearing truth, or filled with truth; probably lies behind a damaged sentence in the explanation of sankalpa — the new transcript captures a similar sound and then “intention filled with truth,” this reading remains provisional and no further claim about Brahman or chitta should be drawn from the damaged sentence. Vikalpa — alternative or conceptual construction; in Yoga Sutra 1.9 it has a specific technical meaning, “doubt” alone does not capture it there. Chitta — mind or mental field, with varying breadth of meaning across traditions, here connected with cintana and chitraṇa. Cintana — thinking, contemplation, deep reflection. Chitraṇa — depicting, imagining, creating a mental image. Vritti — activity, mode, or modification; in this model the domain of analysis and comparison, this assignment differs from Patanjali’s classification. Vishleshaṇa — analysis, analytical breakdown; previously the unclear “mislešan.” Tulana / tulanā — comparison, assessment. Priya–hita–lābha — what is pleasant or dear, benefit or interest, gain; criteria of one side of comparison. Nyāya–dharma–satya — justice, dharma (i.e., right order or right conduct), truth; criteria of the other side of comparison. Saṃskāra — an imprint or disposition arising from experience. Smṛti — memory or recollection; should not commonly be confused with saṃskāra, i.e., “imprint.” Antaḥkaraṇa — the inner instrument, in the common fourfold Vedāntic description includes manas, buddhi, ahaṃkāra, and chitta. Ahaṃkāra — the “I-maker,” identification with the ego, or the appropriation of experience as “I” and “mine.” Saṃvedanā — sensing, sensation, sensitivity; in this conversation connected with the 4½ activities. Saṃjñāna — cognition, awareness, knowing something. Saṃjñānitā — a related form used in the supplied transcript, here denoting the way of living connected with the remaining 5½ activities; it is appropriate to preserve this context and not claim it is a universal technical definition. Mānavīyatā — humanity, humanness; in this explanation, living through all ten activities. Bhoga — enjoyment, use, or experiencing of worldly conditions. Tyāga — renunciation, giving up, relinquishing. Sadupayoga — correct, appropriate, or beneficial use. Samyak — correctly, properly, fully; “balance” is a contextual interpretation. Draṣṭā-bhāva — the attitude of the observer or seer; the earlier Czech rendering “dráštá” did not correctly reflect the vowel lengths. Sākṣī-bhāva — the attitude of the witness; observing in a way in which one does not fully identify with what one observes. Māyā — in the non-dual interpretation given in the conversation, the principle connected with the appearance of separateness or multiplicity. Brahman — the supreme or absolute reality; in the conversation set in contrast to apparent separateness. Cetanā — consciousness, awareness; the speaker does not consider individual and universal consciousness to be qualitatively different. Jñāna — knowledge, understanding. Śravaṇa — listening to the teaching. Manana — contemplating what has been heard. Jñānendriya — a faculty or organ of perception, the traditional five relate to hearing, touch, sight, taste, and smell. Karmendriya — a faculty or organ of action, the traditional five relate to speech, grasping, movement from place to place, excretion, and reproduction. Prāṇa — breath or life force, also the name of one of its five main functional components (prāṇa, apāna, vyāna, udāna, samāna) — the table of ten activities does not set out their detailed functions. Sādhanā — spiritual practice or sustained spiritual development. Dhyāna — meditation. Dhyāna-sādhanā — meditative spiritual practice. Samādhi — meditative absorption; a more precise definition depends on the tradition. Bhakti — devotion. Kūṭastha — that which is unchanging; the earlier conversation connected this term with the area between the eyes, this connection is specific to this teaching and is not a literal definition of the word. Kāraṇa-śarīra — the causal body. Kāraṇa-jagat — the causal world or realm. Prakṛti — nature or primordial nature, depending on context. Śakti — power, capacity, or energy; in some contexts, divine power; should not automatically be taken as a personal name.
